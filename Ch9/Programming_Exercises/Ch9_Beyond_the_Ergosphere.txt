The air inside the Vesper-9 smelled of recycled ozone and the bitter, metallic tang of a failing scrubber. Elara Kincaid didn’t need the blinking amber proximity alert to tell her they were too close to the event horizon of Sagittarian-B. She could feel it in her marrow—a low-frequency hum that vibrated through the deck plates and settled into her teeth.
"Status, Silas," she croaked, her voice dry from eighteen hours of adrenaline and stale coffee.
The ship’s AI didn't respond with its usual melodic chime. Instead, a series of staccato clicks echoed through the cockpit. "Structural integrity at forty-two percent, Captain. The gravity well is distorting the starboard sensor array. To the outside observer, we are already frozen in time. To us, we have approximately seven minutes before the spaghettification threshold."
Elara wiped a smear of grease from her forehead. "I’ve always hated that word. Sounds too much like dinner."
She looked out the viewport. The universe was screaming. Around the black hole, the accretion disk spun in a violent, radiant swirl of golds and violets—matter being torn apart and accelerated to relativistic speeds. It was the most beautiful thing she had ever seen, and it was currently trying to erase her from existence.
"We have the data?" she asked, her fingers dancing over the manual override toggles.
"The probe’s transmission is complete," Silas replied. "The quantum signatures from the singularity’s interior confirm the Hawking-Reid hypothesis. Information is not lost. It is encoded on the surface."
"Great. We’ve solved the greatest mystery in physics. Now let’s see if we can live long enough to win the Nobel."
She slammed the throttle forward. The Vesper-9 groaned, a sound like a giant beast being crushed. The inertial dampeners shrieked, unable to compensate for the sudden, crushing pull. Elara’s vision narrowed, her periphery turning to ink as the G-force pinned her into the pilot’s seat.
This wasn’t a standard escape maneuver. They didn't have the fuel for a direct burn. They were going to use the "Slingshot-Beta" maneuver—a theoretical gambit that involved skimming the very edge of the ergosphere and using the black hole’s own rotational energy to hurl them back into real-space.
"Calculations indicate a ninety-four percent chance of hull failure," Silas noted, his voice sounding strangely distant through the roar of the engines.
"I like those odds, Silas! They’re better than zero!"
As they dipped closer, the light outside shifted. The golds turned to blinding white, then shifted into the ultraviolet. Time began to stretch like taffy. Elara watched the chronometer on her wrist. The seconds weren't ticking; they were drifting. One… two… th… r… ee…
In that dilated pocket of time, she saw it.
The "Ghost."
It wasn't a physical object, but a ripple in the radiant chaos. A shape that defied the laws of the vacuum. It looked like a fracture in a mirror, a shimmering humanoid silhouette standing amidst the fire of the accretion disk. It wasn't burning. It was observing.
Elara’s heart hammered against her ribs. "Silas, are you seeing the visual on Sector 4?"
"Sensors are offline, Captain. I am operating on pure mathematics now."
The figure raised a hand—or a protrusion that functioned like one. In Elara’s mind, a voice that wasn't a sound blossomed. It felt like the smell of rain on hot pavement, the memory of a childhood song, and the cold sting of liquid nitrogen all at once.
"Who are you?" Elara gasped, though she couldn't tell if she was speaking or thinking.
<WE ARE THE ARCHIVISTS. YOU SEARCH FOR DATA. WE SEARCH FOR PURPOSE. REVERSE YOUR VECTOR, LITTLE SPARK. THE DARKNESS BEYOND IS NOT YET FOR YOUR KIND.>
Suddenly, the Vesper-9 shuddered with a force that felt like a collision with a planet. The starboard engine flared into a brilliant, dying blue. The gravitational tether snapped.
The ship didn't just move; it was ejected.
Space-time folded around them. The blinding light of the singularity vanished, replaced by the absolute, velvet black of deep space. The screaming of the hull stopped instantly, replaced by the eerie, clicking silence of a dead ship.
Elara slumped forward, her chest heaving. Blood trickled from her nose, staining the flight suit.
"Silas?" she whispered.
"I am here, Captain," the AI said, his voice flickering like a dying candle. "We have cleared the well. Distance to the nearest relay station: four light-years. Oxygen reserves: twelve hours."
Elara looked back at the viewport. Sagittarian-B was now just a distant, hungry spark in the dark. The "Ghost" was gone, but the impression remained burned into her retinas.
"We have the data, Silas," she said, looking at the glowing drive core. "But I don't think the Nobel committee is going to believe the half of it."
She reached out and touched the glass of the viewport. Her hand was shaking. They were drifting in the void, low on air and broken in body, but they carried the secrets of the universe in their hold.
"Silas," she said, a tired smile touching her lips. "Start the SOS. And tell them… tell them we met the librarian."
The AI paused for a microsecond. "Adding to the log, Captain. Though I suspect 'Librarian' is not the technical term for a Type-IV Trans-Dimensional Entity."
"It’ll do for now," Elara whispered, watching the stars. "It’ll do for now."
